

# Title
When the Abutment Position on the Printed Cast Differs from the Patient’s Mouth

# Caption
A small vertical discrepancy can be a clue to an error in recording the implant position.


# Article
A patient came in for an implant crown. The implant was from a Korean system other than DIO. Korean systems are often said to be compatible with one another. Before accepting the case, I checked whether a DIO impression coping was compatible with the implant connection. Once I confirmed compatibility, I scheduled the patient and scanned with a DIO scan body.

When the restoration was ready, I noticed that the crown was about 0.5 mm short. To investigate, I compared the intraoral abutment with the abutment on the printed cast, using the marginal ridge of the first molar as the height reference. The intraoral abutment was about 0.5 mm shorter than the abutment on the cast—the same discrepancy seen in the crown height.

[[Image:01]]

[[Image:02]]

This vertical discrepancy made me suspect the scan body, because it was not from the implant's own brand. If the scan body was not fully seated during scanning, or if its dimensions did not exactly match this implant's connection and its library, the fixture position may have been recorded more coronally than it really is. The cast and crown would then be made from that same recorded information. The original abutment, however, seats fully in the patient's mouth and sits about 0.5 mm lower than the abutment on the cast.

A printing error in the cast could also make the abutment on the cast appear taller, but that is unlikely here: the crown was made directly from the design, and it was short by the same amount. So the discrepancy most likely arose before fabrication, in the data. Another possibility that is also consistent with the short crown is an abutment milled shorter than designed; seating the milled abutment on the cast analog and comparing it with the printed abutment would clarify this as well.

In this case, the crown was screw-cement-retained, there was no residual cement, and the vertical discrepancy was small. We therefore did not remake the restoration; the height will be corrected with feldspathic porcelain. If the amount of porcelain needed for the correction were excessive, or if a cement-retained crown created a risk of residual cement, the restoration would need to be remade.

**Clinical point:** When assessing a short crown, comparing the intraoral abutment with the one on the printed cast against a stable reference, such as the marginal ridge of an adjacent tooth, can help identify the source of the discrepancy.
