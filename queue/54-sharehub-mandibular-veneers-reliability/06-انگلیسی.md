# Mandibular Veneers: Are They Really as Reliable as Maxillary Ones?

Caption: The enamel of the mandibular incisors is thinner, so do mandibular veneers perform worse? A review of the 3-, 7- and 10-year reports of one study on extended veneers, and the role of exposed dentin.

## Body

The labial enamel of the mandibular incisors is thinner than that of the maxillary incisors; this difference is particularly pronounced in the cervical region [4].

This difference matters clinically. Suppose both arches of a patient are to be treated with ceramic veneers. If reaching the same final shade needs a similar ceramic thickness, dentin is more likely to be exposed in the mandible, because we have less enamel to work with.

On the other hand, we know that dentin exposure can increase the likelihood of veneer complications [1][2][3]. So the initial expectation is that mandibular veneers would perform worse.

But does the clinical evidence confirm this expectation?

## One study; three reports at three time points

The 3-, 7- and 10-year articles we review below are not three independent studies; they are consecutive reports on one group of patients [1][2][3].

In this study, the veneers in both arches were placed by one dentist with a similar treatment protocol. Patients with bruxism were also excluded from the study. So the results should be interpreted in light of these conditions.

## Type of veneers; an important point in interpreting the results

The veneers used in this study were of the Extended type; that is, the ceramic did not cover only the labial surface, but passed over the incisal edge and extended onto the palatal or lingual surface.

In this design, the ceramic coverage could also extend into the proximal areas and, to correct a diastema or displaced teeth or to cover previous restorations, could involve a larger part of the tooth structure.

For this reason, in terms of coverage, these restorations sit between a conventional veneer and a full crown.

The important point is that none of the preparations stayed entirely within enamel, and some dentin was exposed in both arches [2][3].

The researchers assessed the amount of exposed dentin after the final preparation and divided the veneers into two groups depending on whether less or more than 50 percent of the preparation surface involved dentin [1][2][3].

## Results at three, seven and ten years

| Measure | 3 years | 7 years | 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Survival of maxillary veneers | 94.2% | 95% | 91.9% |
| Survival of mandibular veneers | 96.7% | 91.2% | 91.7% |
| Success, maxilla | 91.5% | 82.1% | 74.7% |
| Success, mandible | 94.9% | 88% | 85.7% |
| Increased risk of needing an intervention when more than 50% of dentin is exposed | 10.6 times | 3.71 times | 2.98 times |

To interpret these numbers, we need to know the difference between the two measures:

Survival: the veneer is still in the patient's mouth, even if it has needed an intervention such as recementation during this time. A fracture that leads to replacing the veneer counts as a failure.

Success: a stricter criterion. The veneer has stayed in the mouth without needing any intervention.

In all three reports, no significant difference was seen in the survival of veneers in the two arches [1][2][3].

[[Image:04]]

*Figure 4. Survival probability by jaw in the ten-year report; black: maxilla, red: mandible, horizontal axis: months [3]. Source: Rinke et al., 2020, Figure 5; reproduced with permission, as stated by the founder.*

For success, the picture was somewhat different. The success rate of mandibular veneers was higher at all three time points, and this difference grew over time, so that in the ten-year report the difference came close to the threshold of statistical significance [1][2][3].

[[Image:05]]

*Figure 5. Success (intervention-free) probability by jaw in the ten-year report; black: maxilla, red: mandible [3]. Source: Rinke et al., 2020, Figure 8; reproduced with permission, as stated by the founder.*

So, contrary to the initial expectation, this study shows no evidence of poorer performance of mandibular veneers.

## Exposed dentin; the more important factor in predicting complications

One of the study's important findings was the relationship between the amount of exposed dentin and the need for intervention.

Veneers in which more than half of the preparation surface was in dentin needed intervention more often at all three time points. The most common intervention was recementation, and root canal treatment was also seen in all three reports [1][2][3].

That said, this difference did not necessarily mean lower veneer survival; that is, despite the greater need for intervention, no significant difference was observed in the likelihood of the veneers staying in place or being lost.

The timing of complications is also interesting.

In the group in which more than 50 percent of the preparation surface involved dentin, the first interventions started in the early months and continued in the following years.

In contrast, in the group with less exposed dentin, almost no problems were reported up to about five years, and complications appeared gradually in the later years [3].

[[Image:06]]

*Figure 6. Success probability by amount of exposed dentin in the ten-year report; black: 50% or less, red: more than 50% [3]. Source: Rinke et al., 2020, Figure 9; reproduced with permission, as stated by the founder.*

This pattern shows that the two groups differed not only in the number of complications but also in when they occurred.

Another point to note is the decrease in the risk ratio for needing intervention over time. This figure went from 10.6 times at three years to about 3 times at ten years [1][2][3].

This decrease does not mean that dentin becomes unimportant in the long term. In the early years, the low-dentin group was almost free of complications, and this made the risk ratio look very high. Over time, complications appeared in the low-dentin group too, and the gap between the two groups narrowed.

[[Image:07]]

*Figure 7. Success probability by amount of exposed dentin in the three-year report; black: less than 50%, red: more than 50%, horizontal axis: months [1]. Source: Rinke et al., 2013, Figure 6; reproduced with permission, as stated by the founder.*

Even so, their difference in success still remained by the end of ten years [3].

Clinically, one can take this to mean that problems related to bonding to dentin may manifest earlier, although this interpretation is our reading of the trend in the results, not a direct conclusion of the authors.

## Can we trust the superiority of mandibular veneers?

Despite the study's interesting results, there are several important limitations that should be considered.

1. The amount of exposed dentin is not given separately for each arch.

This is the most important limitation for answering our main question.

We know that the enamel of the mandibular incisors is thinner; but the study does not specify how much of the preparation surface of the maxillary and mandibular veneers actually involved dentin.

So we cannot check whether the thinner enamel of the mandible actually led to more dentin exposure. As a result, the reason for the similar or even better performance of the mandibular veneers remains unclear.

2. Follow-up of the two arches was not exactly the same.

More mandibular veneers dropped out of follow-up during the study, and their follow-up time was also shorter overall [1][2][3].

This matters, because the likelihood of failure and of needing intervention increases over time. So the possibility that this difference in follow-up had an effect cannot be ignored.

3. Some numbers are not consistent between the reports.

For example, the survival of mandibular veneers is reported slightly higher in the ten-year report than in the seven-year report, whereas in a fixed group survival should not increase over time [2][3].

There are also several other inconsistencies in the three-year report [1]. These do not necessarily invalidate the main findings, but they lower the quality of the study's reporting and should be considered when interpreting the results.

## In the end, how reliable are mandibular veneers?

This study does not show that mandibular veneers perform worse than maxillary ones. Their success rate was even reported higher at all three time points.

But these results are not enough to prove that mandibular veneers are superior either.

The more important point is that in both arches, more exposed dentin was associated with a greater need for intervention.

So in clinical decision-making, merely whether the arch is upper or lower should not be the main criterion for assessing risk. The amount of remaining enamel, the extent of the preparation and the share of exposed dentin remain very important.

Clinical message: a mandibular veneer is not necessarily a weaker or more complication-prone treatment; but preserving as much enamel as possible and limiting dentin exposure remain among the most important controllable factors for increasing treatment success.
