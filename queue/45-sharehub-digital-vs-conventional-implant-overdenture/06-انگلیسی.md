# Digital Overdentures: Do They Really Give the Patient a Better Experience?

Caption: In a crossover trial where each patient used both prostheses for a year, what difference did a fully digital workflow for implant overdentures make for the patient, and how much of it comes down to the design of the bar and attachment?

## Body

Suppose we have an edentulous patient whose maxillary ridge is severely resorbed and who is not happy with their complete denture either. One of the options we have is an implant overdenture. But we know the job is not over once the implants are placed: which attachment we choose, how we design the bar and which method we use to make the prosthesis all affect the result.

The question that comes up here is this: if we do all these steps digitally, does only our way of working change, or does the patient really get a better prosthesis too?

A clinical trial published in 2025 looked at exactly this question. What makes the study interesting is that every patient tried both prostheses, that is, lived for one year with a conventional overdenture and for one year with a digital overdenture. The patients all had the situation I described: their maxilla was severely resorbed and they were not happy with their denture. First they received a bone graft, and then four to six implants were placed in the maxilla.

In the digital method, instead of taking impressions, the patient's existing denture and the implants were scanned and the prosthesis was designed in software. The bar was titanium, retention of the prosthesis came from a PEEK component on the bar, and the base and teeth were milled as well. The whole job was done in three sessions, whereas the conventional method took five or six sessions. In the conventional group, most patients had Locators in the maxilla.

So, did the patients feel a difference or not?

[[+key]]Let me say first that both types of overdenture, conventional and digital alike, were much better than the complete denture.[[+/key]] Patient satisfaction with the complete denture was about 25 out of 100, and with the overdenture it rose to about 80 to 90. That means most of this improvement is due to the implant overdenture itself, not to its being digital.

But if we compare the two methods with each other, digital was a little better. Patient satisfaction was about 7 points out of 100 higher, and the difference was seen mostly in the prosthesis's function, pain and comfort. So the difference is real, but it is not large. One interesting point is that the patients could not tell from the look of the prosthesis which one was digital, but at the end of the study, when they were asked to choose one to keep for good, roughly two out of every three chose the digital one.

Prosthetic problems were also fewer with the digital method, especially in the maxilla. But this has to be read with caution, because many of the conventional group's problems related to the Locator itself, for example replacing the nylon ring, which is more part of this system's routine maintenance than a treatment failure. These numbers were also only counted, and no statistical analysis was done on them.

But the main point is that this advantage cannot all be credited to the digital workflow. The two groups did not differ only in the fabrication method; their attachment type and prosthesis design differed as well. The authors themselves link the reason mainly to the design, that is, the good retention of the PEEK, the slimmer prosthesis and the fact that less of the palate was covered. So an important part of this result may be due to the bar and the attachment, not to the scan.

> [[+]]
>
> Two practical points also come out of this study. First, ten of the patients could not get a digital prosthesis for the mandible, because their implant brand was not in the software's library. That means whether we can work digitally depends on the implant the patient already has. Second, the jaw relation is taken from the patient's existing denture, so that denture must still have correct occlusion.

They saw one more thing: in the second year, patients were more satisfied, whichever prosthesis they had. Probably because in the first year they were still getting used to an implant-borne prosthesis. So the passage of time and the patient getting used to it may also affect their satisfaction.

> [[+]]
>
> So if I want to sum up, the digital workflow works for overdentures, and in this study, with fewer sessions, it produced a prosthesis that patients were at least as satisfied with as the conventional method, and probably a little more. But this advantage is small, it is not clear how much of it belongs to the digital workflow and how much to the prosthesis design, and it was seen in patients who had a particular situation. Whether the digital method reduces cost has also not yet been properly studied.

That is why choosing the method still depends on the case itself: the implant the patient has, the space we have, the attachment that suits that case, and the facilities we and our laboratory have. [[+key]]Digital is useful when it makes the work more predictable in that case, not just because it is the newer method.[[+/key]]
