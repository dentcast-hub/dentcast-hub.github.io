# The Implant as an RPD Abutment (ICRPD): When and How to Use It

Caption: When is an implant surveyed crown a suitable abutment for a removable partial denture, and how should implant position, prosthesis design and maintenance be planned? A clinical guide based on a 2024 systematic review.

## Body

## Why add an implant to a partial denture design?

One of the problems of removable partial dentures, especially in distal-extension cases, is movement of the prosthesis during chewing. The reason is that the abutment tooth and the mucosa under the denture base do not behave the same way under load. The tooth is supported by the periodontal ligament, whereas the prosthesis base rests on the mucosa and ridge and is displaced more.

The fewer teeth remain, the harder it becomes to control the movement of the prosthesis and to provide its retention and support.

In such situations, we do not always need to move toward extensive fixed treatment. Sometimes, by placing one or more implants in a suitable position, the support and retention of the partial denture can be improved. This approach is called Implant-Assisted Removable Partial Denture (IARPD).

There are two main approaches to this treatment:

> **1. Using attachments**
>
> The implant is placed under the prosthesis and connected to the denture with attachments such as Locator, ball or magnet. In effect, the treatment logic is close to that of an implant overdenture.

> **2. Using a surveyed crown (ICRPD)**
>
> A crown or bridge is made on the implant and designed like an RPD abutment tooth; that is, it has a Rest Seat and a controlled undercut, and the prosthesis clasp sits on it.

In this approach, the implant plays the role of an abutment tooth, and the partial denture is made with the usual principles of RPD design, including Rest and clasp.

From here on, our focus is on the second approach, ICRPD.

## Which patients are suitable candidates?

This approach can be considered in patients who:

- have a limited number of remaining teeth and whose partial denture, especially in distal-extension areas, moves a lot or lacks adequate retention.
- cannot have, or do not want, extensive fixed treatment; for example because of bone conditions, cost or the patient's treatment limitations.
- have a markedly resorbed posterior ridge, so that placing an implant in the posterior region would require a bone graft or sinus lift.

Of course, the choice of approach also depends on the jaw and the patient's priorities. In the studies, esthetic satisfaction was apparently higher with the implant overdenture in both jaws. Functionally, ICRPD gave greater satisfaction in the mandible, and no difference was seen in the maxilla.

## Implant position: more important than the number of implants

[[+key]]An important point in this treatment is that we must first design the partial denture and then determine the implant position, not the other way round.[[+/key]]

The path of insertion, the Rest location, the clasp position and the major connector design must be defined so that we can choose the best implant position. Therefore, merely suitable bone or easier surgery should not determine where the implant goes.

### First preference: the anterior or premolar region, adjacent to a natural tooth

In this review, implants placed in the anterior region next to a natural tooth showed the highest survival and success rates.

One of the common and successful combinations was an implant in the anterior or premolar region used as a surveyed crown, together with a Kennedy Class I distal-extension partial denture.

This position can reduce the need for bone grafting and complex surgical treatment and, as a result, shorten treatment time and lower its cost.

### Why can placing an implant only at the posterior end of the arch be problematic?

Several biomechanical points matter here:

- Unlike a natural tooth, an implant has no periodontal ligament and behaves differently under lateral forces.
- The clasp loads the implant at crown level, at a distance from the bone surface. This distance can generate considerable torque.
- When the only implant is at the posterior end of the arch, much of the horizontal movement of the prosthesis and the forces it produces are transferred to that implant.

In the available reports, failures of posterior implants were seen mostly at the terminal positions of the arch.

### Is a posterior implant the wrong choice?

No. The survival rate of posterior implants has been reported at about 97 percent, and placing an implant in the first molar region can even be biomechanically beneficial.

But not all posterior positions are equal. In some studies, moving the implant from the last molar to the premolar region resulted in better force distribution.

So the question is not simply whether the implant is anterior or posterior; what matters is its position relative to the remaining teeth and the partial denture design.

### The number of abutments also matters

As the number of abutments increased, the failure rate was lower. By contrast, the risk of failure or complications was reported to be higher in the following situations:

- Kennedy Class II partial dentures compared with Class I
- the maxilla compared with the mandible
- simultaneous treatment of both jaws

## Principles of prosthesis design

In this approach, the implant crown must be treated as part of the partial denture design, not just an ordinary crown on which a clasp is later placed.

Several points are especially important in the design:

### 1. The major connector should be rigid

In successful cases, a rigid major connector was a common feature. A rigid connector helps control the movement of the different parts of the prosthesis better, so that less of the force from denture displacement is transferred to the implant.

### 2. Design the implant crown like an abutment tooth

The crown should have an adequate Rest Seat and a controlled undercut. The clasp should also be designed so that, while providing adequate retention, it does not apply unnecessary lateral forces to the implant.

### 3. Choose the cement carefully

One of the common complications in the studies was dislodgement of the surveyed crown due to washout of temporary cement. Therefore, temporary cement is not a suitable choice for these crowns, and a more durable cementation method should be used.

## What outcomes can we expect from treatment?

Based on the studies reviewed, the overall results were as follows:

- **Implant survival:** about 95 to 100 percent
- **Implant success:** about 70 to 90 percent
- **Marginal bone loss:** usually limited; in some comparisons lower than with attachments, but not in all studies.
- **Overall complications:** fewer than with the attachment-based approach.
- **Patient satisfaction:** improved after treatment.

> [[+]]
>
> The important point is that implant survival should not be equated with implant success. An implant may still remain in the mouth while bone loss or inflammation of the surrounding tissue has taken it outside the success criteria.

## Common complications and maintenance points

In this treatment, a considerable share of the problems relates to the prosthetic components, not the implant itself.

| Complication | Clinical action |
|---|---|
| Crown dislodgement due to washout of temporary cement | Use a suitable cement and check crown stability at recall |
| Loss of clasp retention (about 33 to 43 percent in Class I) | Periodic check and adjustment of the clasp |
| Fracture or deformation of the framework, Rest or clasp | Check the framework and Rest contact at recall visits |
| Screw loosening or fracture | Check the stability of the screw and prosthetic components |
| Fracture of the veneer, denture tooth or opposing tooth | Check and control the occlusion |
| Mucosal sore spots | Check and relieve pressure areas of the denture base |

### Do not forget the peri-implant tissues

In the studies, peri-implant mucositis was reported at about 18 percent and peri-implantitis at about 9 percent.

Interestingly, calculus accumulation on the surveyed crowns was about 30 percent, whereas in the attachment-based approach it was reported at about 4 percent.

The Rest Seat and undercut on the implant crown can create a favourable site for plaque accumulation. For this reason, hygiene instruction tailored to the prosthesis design is very important.

Examination of the peri-implant soft tissue, probing and periodic radiographs should be part of these patients' maintenance programme.

## How strong is the evidence?

Despite the promising results, we should be cautious in interpreting these findings.

Most of the evidence comes from retrospective studies and case reports, and all the clinical studies included were from South Korea. In addition, the quality of the studies was not assessed, and there were no controlled clinical trials in this body of evidence.

Therefore, ICRPD can be considered a reasonable and practical treatment option for some patients, but we do not yet have enough evidence to establish its superiority over attachment-based approaches.

## Clinical summary

> In ICRPD, the implant is not merely an additional abutment for the partial denture; it must be considered part of the prosthesis design from the start.
>
> Choosing a suitable implant position based on the partial denture design, using a rigid major connector, designing the surveyed crown correctly and attending to periodic maintenance appear to be important factors in the success of this treatment.
>
> Without converting the whole treatment into a fixed prosthesis, this approach can improve the support and retention of the partial denture, especially in patients with a limited number of abutment teeth and a resorbed posterior ridge.
