# Digital Complete Dentures: Milling or 3D Printing?

Caption: Once the impressions and jaw relations are recorded, what does the choice of fabrication method change for the patient? In part four of the removable prosthodontics series, we review two studies through the lens of retention, patient experience, and appointment count.

## Body

Suppose a patient comes in for complete dentures. We can fabricate them conventionally, mill them from a resin block, or produce them with a 3D printer. Which method should we choose?

Patients generally care little about the manufacturing method. They want dentures that stay in place during speech, let them eat, look natural, and feel comfortable.

The question is whether the fabrication method makes a difference to how the denture performs and what the patient experiences.

We will look at two papers. One is a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing milled with 3D-printed dentures. The other is a clinical study comparing milled with conventional dentures.

A digital denture can still involve conventional impressions and jaw relation records. The digital steps may be limited to design and fabrication.

In conventional fabrication, polymerization of the acrylic resin can cause dimensional changes that affect base adaptation.

In milling, the denture is machined from a prepolymerized resin block. This reduces polymerization-related dimensional change during fabrication, though milling has limitations when reproducing certain complex surfaces.

3D printing builds the denture layer by layer and offers more flexibility in reproducing complex shapes. Final accuracy may depend on printer settings and post-processing.

A difference in fabrication does not necessarily translate into a difference in clinical performance.

Retention is an important measure of denture performance. It describes the denture’s resistance to forces that dislodge it from its seat.

Retention also depends on border extension, the peripheral seal, saliva, and denture design. Retention and stability are distinct. A well-retained denture can still move under lateral forces.

In 2025, Khorasani and colleagues published a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing milled and 3D-printed complete dentures for retention and patient satisfaction.

There is limited evidence comparing milled and 3D-printed dentures. Three studies assessed retention and two assessed patient satisfaction. When choosing a fabrication method, we should interpret each result in light of the small number of studies behind it.

Mean retention was slightly higher for 3D-printed dentures, but the difference was statistically nonsignificant.

One might expect milled dentures to have better retention because they use prepolymerized resin. The clinical evidence did not show such an advantage.

The authors suggest that 3D printing may reproduce complex denture-base surfaces more readily. This explanation was not directly tested.

There was substantial heterogeneity, and retention measurements and fabrication details varied across studies.

The available evidence shows no clear retention advantage for either approach. A statistically nonsignificant difference does not establish equivalence.

Patient satisfaction adds another perspective.

While mean retention slightly favoured 3D printing, mean patient satisfaction tended to favour milling. That difference was also statistically nonsignificant, and heterogeneity was even greater.

[[تصویر:02]]

These results illustrate that higher retention does not necessarily translate into greater patient satisfaction.

A patient may be happy with retention but still have difficulty speaking or be dissatisfied with tooth arrangement, lip support, or occlusion.

The authors suggest that future studies assess satisfaction in separate domains, including comfort, stability, speech, appearance, and occlusion.

Most of the included studies focused on maxillary dentures. The differences in supporting area and muscular control make it difficult to generalize the findings to mandibular dentures.

Direct comparisons of milling and 3D printing still provide too little evidence to establish that either approach performs better clinically.

How do digital and conventional dentures compare?

In 2026, Rechlin and colleagues conducted a randomized crossover pilot study of 15 edentulous patients. Each patient received both conventional and milled complete dentures and wore each set for three months.

The digital protocol retained conventional impressions, conventional preliminary jaw relation records, and a try-in.

The digital workflow preserved core clinical procedures. The main difference lay in design and fabrication.

[[تصویر:03]]

[[تصویر:04]]

The digital protocol required four fabrication appointments, compared with six for the conventional protocol. That could matter to patients who have difficulty travelling or tolerating repeated appointments.

Those appointment counts apply to this study protocol. They cannot be generalized to every digital system.

The study reported improved oral health-related quality of life from baseline with both approaches.

The median questionnaire score was 16 for milled dentures and 19 for conventional dentures. A lower score means the patient reported fewer problems. The difference was not statistically significant, and it is unclear whether patients would notice a three-point difference in everyday life.

[[تصویر:05]]

A change in questionnaire score may not correspond to a change that patients notice in everyday life.

The researchers also assessed retention, stability, base adaptation, border extension, occlusion, appearance, and speech.

Both denture types received acceptable overall ratings, with no statistically significant difference.

[[تصویر:06]]

One patient could not tolerate the conventional denture and was highly satisfied with the milled denture. This individual result cannot establish that milled dentures are generally better tolerated.

Both denture types required occlusal and pressure-spot adjustments after delivery, with a similar extent of adjustment.

Digital fabrication still requires checking border extension, tissue pressure, and occlusal contacts.

This was a small pilot study with three months of wear per denture. The clinical assessor knew the fabrication method, and questionnaire data were incomplete.

The study cannot establish long-term durability or definitive equivalence.

What do these findings mean for clinical decisions?

Milling and 3D printing differ in how they manufacture dentures, while evidence for a clear advantage in retention or patient satisfaction remains insufficient.

Milled dentures had short-term clinical performance comparable to conventional dentures in the Rechlin study. The digital protocol required fewer appointments, and the study detected no statistically significant difference in quality of life or overall clinical scores.

Retention, stability, and patient satisfaction describe distinct outcomes. A denture that takes more force to dislodge may still be uncomfortable.

A reliable digital workflow with fewer visits can be a reasonable option for patients who have difficulty travelling. For patients with severely resorbed ridges, complex tissue conditions, or limited muscular control, these two studies cannot establish that milling or printing will necessarily yield a better result.

Keeping a digital file may allow the denture to be reproduced later. These studies did not evaluate the clinical impact of that capability. Fewer visits do not automatically mean lower total treatment costs.

The choice between conventional, milled, and printed dentures starts with the patient’s circumstances and a reliable workflow.

Impressions, jaw relation records, base design, tooth position, and occlusal adjustment remain important regardless of the fabrication method.

Technology can change how we fabricate a denture. The evidence here has yet to show that a change in fabrication alone necessarily improves clinical outcomes.
