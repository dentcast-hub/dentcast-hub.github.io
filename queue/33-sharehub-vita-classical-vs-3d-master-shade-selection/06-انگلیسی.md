# Title

Tooth Shade Selection: How Do VITA Classical and 3D-MASTER Differ?

# Caption

A comparison of VITA Classical and 3D-MASTER, from shade arrangement and the three dimensions of color to the steps of tooth shade selection.

# Body

Many of us have worked with the **VITA Classical** shade guide for years, and shades such as A2 and A3 are very familiar. Yet we may have given less thought to how these shades were selected, or why, even after repeatedly holding the tabs beside a patient's tooth, none seems to match its color exactly.

VITA has two well-known systems: **VITA Classical A1–D4** and **VITA SYSTEM 3D-MASTER**. Their difference goes beyond the number of shade tabs: their design principles differ. Understanding that difference can help us select shades better and recognize the limitations of the guide itself.

## First, understand the three dimensions of color

Tooth color can be described in three dimensions:

**Value** is how light or dark a color is. **Chroma** describes its intensity or saturation; for example, two teeth may have a similar yellow hue, but one may be a more intense yellow. **Hue** is the underlying color family toward which a color tends.

Natural tooth shades occupy only a small part of color space, with hues predominantly between yellow and red. Every shade guide effectively tries to cover this small range with a defined number of color samples.

## VITA Classical: an empirically selected set of shades

Classical contains **16 shades** in four families, A through D. In VITA's own description, family **A** is reddish-brownish, **B** reddish-yellowish, **C** greyish, and **D** reddish-grey.

These shades were selected empirically: they were not originally intended to be equally spaced in color space. VITA's diagram shows that some shades lie very close together, while others are separated by larger gaps.

The clinical implication matters: **we may have correctly selected the nearest shade-guide tab, yet that tab may still not match the patient's tooth exactly.** This does not necessarily mean our judgment was wrong; the actual tooth shade may lie between the guide's samples.

A second issue is that Classical tabs are primarily grouped by Hue families rather than by a systematic Value sequence. The numbers within each family do not necessarily represent a fixed step in lightness or saturation either.

Therefore, if we want to compare **Value** first with Classical, it is better to arrange the tabs from light to dark. One established teaching sequence is:

**B1, A1, B2, D2, A2, C1, C2, D4, A3, D3, B3, A3.5, B4, C3, A4, C4**

This list is not provided as text in the brochure used here; it comes from a supplementary educational source. However, the brochure's diagram also places **B1 lighter than A1**.

The interesting point is that **D2, despite its greyish hue, is lighter than A2 in this sequence.** A greyer color does not necessarily have a lower Value.

(In my view, confusing hue with lightness can mislead us when selecting the shade of a single crown. A shade that looks greyer may not actually be darker in Value.)

## 3D-MASTER: organizing color space

VITA took a different approach in designing 3D-MASTER. Instead of an empirical set of samples with an uneven distribution, it placed shades in a more systematic arrangement within natural tooth color space. According to the manufacturer's brochure, this system has **26 natural shades** and **three shades for bleached teeth**.

3D-MASTER first has five main **Value** groups, from **1, the lightest**, to **5, the darkest**. Within each group, **Chroma** increases from top to bottom, while **Hue** is identified by three letters: **L**, more yellowish; **M**, intermediate; and **R**, more reddish.

For example, in **3M2**, the number 3 identifies the Value group, M indicates the intermediate Hue, and 2 denotes the Chroma level. Each shade code therefore conveys information about all three dimensions.

This arrangement offers two benefits. First, it provides a clearer selection pathway, without having to search among all the samples from the outset. Second, **intermediate shades** can be defined more precisely and produced using suitable material mixtures. The brochure describes this possibility, but **does not specify a fixed mixing ratio, such as 50:50, for any arbitrary pair of shades**.

(In my view, the main advantage of 3D-MASTER is not simply its larger number of shades. More importantly, it makes shade selection a more systematic process instead of a relatively empirical comparison. This organization alone does not guarantee a better final shade match for every restoration.)

## Three-step shade selection with 3D-MASTER

**Step one: Value.** First, compare the intermediate (M) tabs from different groups beside the tooth and choose the group closest in lightness. VITA itself considers Value the most relevant dimension in shade determination and therefore places it first.

**Step two: Chroma.** Once the Value group has been selected, compare the M tabs within that group to determine the tooth's color intensity or saturation.

**Step three: Hue.** Finally, compare the selected tab with the L and R shades to determine whether the tooth is more yellowish or reddish than the intermediate shade.

Instead of deciding lightness, intensity and hue simultaneously, we determine one dimension at a time.

## A few points we forget during shade selection

Regardless of the shade guide, selection conditions matter. VITA recommends using standardized **daylight** illumination whenever possible, keeping surrounding colors neutral, removing lipstick and makeup that affect shade assessment, and covering brightly colored clothing with a grey drape.

Viewing time is another consideration. According to the VITA brochure, the eye tires after approximately **5–7 seconds** of shade assessment. It therefore recommends making the selection quickly and, when uncertain, trusting the initial impression rather than prolonging the comparison continuously. The shade guide should also be disinfected after each use.

## Which is better in the end?

Classical remains useful, especially if we recognize that its shades are not evenly spaced in color space and rearrange its tabs when comparing Value. With its more systematic arrangement and three-step method, 3D-MASTER also gives greater structure to shade selection and communication with the laboratory.

For me, however, the most important message is this: **choosing the nearest shade is different from choosing an exact color match.** Sometimes a patient's tooth color has no exact equivalent among the samples. In that situation, knowing whether the main difference lies in Value, Chroma or Hue becomes more important than simply reporting a shade code.

A shade guide is not meant to judge color on our behalf. It is meant to help us describe what we see more clearly and communicate it to the laboratory more accurately.
