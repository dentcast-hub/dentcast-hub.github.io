# Does a Darker Articulating Paper Mark Mean More Force?

Caption: What do the size and darkness of an articulating paper mark show, and what don't they? A look at a 2026 review on articulating paper, shimstock and T-Scan.

## Body

When checking the occlusion, most of us see a large, dark mark on a crown, assume that this is where the greatest force lands, and adjust that very spot. A review article published in 2026 in Dentistry Journal surveys the methods of occlusal assessment and shows that this assumption is not reliable.

According to the studies this article reviews, the size and darkness of an articulating paper mark do not reliably correspond to the amount of force. In one of these studies, the largest mark was on the tooth that actually carried the highest force in only about 38 percent of cases. So the paper tells us where the contact is, but not how strong it is or whether it occurs before the others.

[[تصویر:01]]

Paper thickness matters too. Thin papers (8 to 12 microns) show fine contacts without altering the occlusal relationship and are better suited to precise prosthetic adjustment. Thick papers (80 to 200 microns), by contrast, leave broader marks, make the contact area look larger than it really is, and may lead us into unnecessary grinding. Shimstock, for its part, only confirms whether a contact is present or not and says nothing about its intensity.

[[تصویر:02]]

Digital systems such as T-Scan fill exactly this gap and record force distribution and contact timing, but the article regards them as complementary, not as a replacement. Most of them show relative force, still need paper to locate the exact contact on the tooth, and it is not yet clear that their additional data improve treatment outcomes.

[[تصویر:03]]

So articulating paper remains the main tool of everyday practice, provided we ask it only for the location of the contact. When we compare contacts before and after treatment, the criterion is which contacts are present and where they are, not which mark has become larger.
