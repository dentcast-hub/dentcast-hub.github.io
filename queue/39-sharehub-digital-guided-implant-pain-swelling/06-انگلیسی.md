# Digital Implants: Do They Really Cause No Pain or Swelling?

Caption: Do digital implants or guided surgery reduce pain and swelling after surgery? A look at the scientific evidence on the role of the surgical guide, flapless surgery and pain after implant placement.

## Body

These days, Instagram ads often claim that "digital implants cause no pain or swelling." I saw one of these clips too, and went to the scientific evidence to see how true the claim is.

## What does "digital implant" mean?

It usually means computer-assisted implant surgery (CAIS): the implant is planned on a CBCT and a scan, and then placed with a static guide or with dynamic navigation. But being digital does not, by itself, decide whether a flap is raised. With a guide you can raise a flap or work flapless. This difference matters a great deal for pain and swelling.

## Does the guide alone reduce pain?

When guided surgery is also done with a flap, what patients report during surgery and in the first days of tissue healing does not differ significantly from the conventional approach [2]. A systematic review that compared static guides with freehand placement also found no tangible difference in discomfort during or after surgery, though with low-quality evidence [3].

In a trial on single posterior teeth, the static guide did not reduce postoperative pain [4]. Dynamic navigation, although it placed the implant more accurately, had no effect on pain or analgesic use [5]. The abstracts of these two studies do not say whether a flap was raised, so all we can conclude from them is that the guide showed no advantage for pain here.

## So where does the reported reduction in pain come from?

From the surgery being flapless. A meta-analysis that compared flapless guided surgery with freehand surgery with a flap showed less pain in the flapless group, but the size of this reduction is uncertain and its certainty is low. The difference in swelling in the same meta-analysis was not significant either [1].

The problem is that in this comparison both the guide and the flap changed, so we cannot say which of them reduced the pain.

One study separated the two and compared three groups: conventional surgery, a bone-supported guide (which needs a flap), and a flapless mucosa-supported guide. The flapless group had less pain than both other groups and used fewer analgesics. The bone-supported guide did not have the same advantage [6].

Another review cites an older systematic review saying that the reduction in pain after guided surgery is probably due to the surgery being flapless itself. But this review's own conclusion was that, based on two trials, there was no significant difference between the two approaches and that pain reduction is still under discussion. The same review also mentions a trial in which the flapless guided group had worse results than open-flap surgery [7]. So even the advantage of flapless surgery has not been replicated everywhere.

## Summary of the studies

None of these studies showed that the guide alone reduces pain. Wherever a reduction in pain was seen, the group was flapless guided surgery.

| Study | What was compared | Result for pain and swelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Romandini 2022 [1] | Flapless guided vs freehand with a flap (meta-analysis) | Less pain with low certainty; difference in swelling not significant |
| Pimkhaokham 2022 [2] | Guide with a flap vs conventional approach (review) | Patient experience in the first days, when the guide was used with a flap, did not differ significantly |
| Tattan 2020 [3] | Static guide vs freehand (systematic review) | No tangible difference in discomfort (low-quality evidence) |
| Kunavisarut 2022 [4] | Static guide vs conventional, single posterior tooth | No significant difference in pain |
| Jorba-García 2023 [5] | Dynamic navigation vs freehand | Similar pain and analgesic use |
| Arısan 2010 [6] | Flapless guide, bone-supported guide (with a flap) and conventional approach | The flapless group had less pain and analgesic use than the other two groups |
| Colombo 2017 [7] | Guide vs conventional approach (review of two trials) | Found no significant difference; considered pain reduction under discussion |

## But with digital, flapless work is more likely, isn't it?

That is true. When we do not open a flap, we cannot see the bone, and in my view 3D planning and a guide help a great deal in making sure the implant ends up in bone and in the right position. A critical review said the same: the guide alone apparently does not consistently improve outcomes, but to the extent that it increases the use of flapless surgery, it can indirectly improve outcomes [2].

But two points should be kept in mind:

- Not every patient is a candidate for flapless surgery. When bone width is insufficient, or bone grafting or soft-tissue management is needed, a flap has to be raised, digital or not.
- Flapless is not painless either. Even in the study where the flapless group had the best result, patients in this group took four analgesic tablets [6].

## Conclusion

The accurate statement is that minimally invasive (flapless) surgery probably reduces pain in the first days, and digital planning can make it more predictable. But the claim that "digital implants cause no pain or swelling" does not fit the scientific evidence, neither for pain nor for swelling.
