Ceramic Laminate Veneers: Which Material, and What to Expect from Longevity?

فارسی

Ceramic laminate veneers are one of the minimally invasive and reliable options for restoring anterior teeth and premolars. But when it comes to longevity, several questions arise: does the type of ceramic make a difference to how long the treatment lasts? Which complications are seen more often over time? And which factors can increase the likelihood of treatment failure?

A systematic review and meta-analysis, whose literature search ran until February 2024, examined the performance of laminate veneers made of feldspathic ceramic, leucite-reinforced glass-ceramic, lithium disilicate and zirconia.

According to the results of this study, the survival rate of laminate veneers at a mean follow-up of 10.4 years was 96.05 percent. In addition, the studies that had examined patient satisfaction were generally able to show an increase in satisfaction after treatment.

1. How do the materials differ?

In terms of veneer survival at the 10.4-year follow-up, no significant difference was seen between the three main ceramics:

  • Feldspathic: 96.13 percent
  • Leucite-reinforced glass-ceramic: 93.7 percent
  • Lithium disilicate: 96.81 percent

Therefore, based on this study, it cannot be said that one of these three materials has a clear advantage in terms of treatment survival. But when we turn to complications, the differences become more apparent.

Over the same period, the technical complication rate was reported as 41.48 percent for feldspathic, 29.87 percent for leucite and 6.1 percent for lithium disilicate. Biological complications were also lower in the lithium disilicate group.

These numbers, however, do not mean that the veneer was lost or needed to be replaced. Many of the complications were managed without replacing the restoration. Even so, the results show that lithium disilicate was associated with fewer technical and biological complications in this review.

This difference should be interpreted with caution, because the comparison is based on non-randomized and heterogeneous studies and cannot be taken as the definitive superiority of one material.

As for zirconia, one study with a mean follow-up of 2.6 years reported 100 percent survival with no complications. But in this review there was still no study with long-term follow-up for zirconia; therefore this result cannot be generalized to the long-term longevity of zirconia.

2. Which complications are seen more often over time?

In the first years after treatment, biological and esthetic complications are not very common and most problems are technical, including chipping and marginal gap formation.

At longer follow-ups, marginal gaps and discoloration in the marginal area are seen more often. One possible explanation for this finding is gingival recession over time, which can expose the margin and lead to leakage at the bonding interface.

Not every marginal change means treatment failure. In the studies of this review, marginal gaps and marginal discoloration rarely led to replacement of the veneer.

Complications that truly require a new restoration were seen less often. At the 10.4-year follow-up, the rate of bulk fracture was reported as 2.51 percent and debonding as 2.2 percent. Secondary caries was 2.83 percent and endodontic complications 0.96 percent.

3. Preparation: preserve enamel as far as possible

One of the points that stands out in some of the studies of this review is that veneers bonded to a larger area of dentin had more complications over time. For this reason, bonding to dentin is raised as one of the possible risk factors for failure.

Other systematic reviews cited by the authors have also shown that bonding to enamel, especially in the marginal area, and the presence of an incisal overlap are associated with a lower failure rate.

These findings show the importance of preserving enamel in the design and preparation of teeth for laminate veneers.

If the clinical situation allows the use of a laminate veneer, a full-coverage crown is not necessarily a better choice. In another systematic review, biological complications in anterior single crowns were reported to be 50 percent higher than in partial restorations. The authors attribute this difference to the more invasive preparation for crowns.

4. Bruxism: the role of the occlusal guard

Bruxism can also affect the likelihood of veneer failure. In one of the studies of this review, the risk of veneer fracture in patients with bruxism was reported to be twice that of patients without bruxism.

Among patients with bruxism, the risk of fracture in those who did not use a splint was seven times that of those who had a splint.

Clinical note

These results, of course, come from a single study; even so, using an occlusal guard after cementation of laminate veneers in patients with parafunctional habits can be beneficial. Regular recall and maintenance care can also help preserve the treatment result over time.
