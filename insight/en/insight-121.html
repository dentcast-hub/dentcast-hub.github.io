Insight 121 — Zirconia Veneers: Should Bonding Weakness Be Compensated With More Preparation?

Published: 2026-11-24 Last reviewed: 2026-11-24
فارسی

Recently, a promotional video by a prosthodontist made claims about low-cost veneers. The gist was that these veneers are usually made of zirconia—described in the video as a low-quality white metal. Because it allegedly bonds poorly, more tooth surface must be prepared to gain retention, and this wider preparation could eventually lead to extensive decay.

At first glance the argument may sound reasonable, but when its parts are examined separately, several incorrect premises and conclusions turn out to be stacked on top of each other.

1. Zirconia is not a metal

Zirconia, or zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂), is an oxide ceramic, not a metal. The confusion probably arises from the similarity of its name to the element zirconium, which is a metal.

Zirconia has been used in dentistry for years to make crowns, bridges and other restorations. That does not mean it is suitable for every type of restoration; but a material cannot be judged low quality simply because some of its products are inexpensive.

Conversely, a low price alone is no basis for a conclusion about a veneer's composition. Such a claim requires the actual product specifications or manufacturer information.

2. Does zirconia bond poorly?

Zirconia differs from ceramics such as lithium disilicate, and one of the important differences concerns the bonding protocol.

It is true that zirconia, unlike glass-ceramics, is not etched with hydrofluoric acid; but that does not mean a proper bond cannot be created. Its bonding relies on methods such as controlled sandblasting and an MDP-containing primer.

Therefore, the limitation of one surface-preparation method cannot be read as an absolute weakness of zirconia bonding.

Clinical tip

More importantly, a ceramic veneer is a restoration that relies on bonding, not on mechanical retention. Widening the preparation solely to gain more retention is therefore not a suitable answer to bonding limitations.

In fact, that decision can backfire.

3. Why does enamel preservation matter in veneers?

Extending preparation into proximal surfaces and increasing its depth raises the likelihood of passing through enamel into dentin, while resin bonding to enamel is usually more predictable and durable than bonding to dentin.

So when we widen the preparation to increase mechanical retention, we may undermine one of the most important factors in veneer success: preserving the enamel substrate.

4. Do these preparations necessarily cause decay?

No. More preparation alone is not the cause of decay. Caries is a multifactorial process that depends on factors such as plaque control, margin status and the biological conditions of the tooth.

Nevertheless, unnecessary and aggressive preparation can destroy healthy tooth structure and complicate treatment. The main concern with this approach is therefore not only decay; it is also the loss of healthy tissue, the reduced enamel surface available for bonding, and a higher risk of restoration failure.

Conclusion

The video first assumes, without presenting specific evidence, that low-cost veneers are made of zirconia. On that assumption it attributes properties such as being a metal, low quality and poor bonding to the material, and finally concludes that more preparation is needed to compensate for this weakness.

But when the starting point of an argument is unproven or incorrect, the conclusions that follow have no scientific validity either.

Bottom line

Zirconia is an oxide ceramic, not a metal; its bonding has its own dedicated protocol, and bonding limitations cannot be compensated for by excessive preparation and mechanical retention. In veneer treatment, preserving enamel is a foundational principle of preparation design. If using a material requires sacrificing more tooth structure, the choice of material—and the indication for treatment itself—must be reconsidered.

Criticising low-cost veneers and improper treatment is necessary, but that criticism should not rest on attributing incorrect properties to a material or on generating fear about it. This is especially important when such claims serve to introduce or promote another treatment approach.

Material selection and preparation design should rest on scientific evidence and clinical conditions, not on a chain of reasoning that begins with an unproven premise and ends in a promotional conclusion.
